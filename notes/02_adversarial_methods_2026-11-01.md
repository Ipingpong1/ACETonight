# Adversarial methodological and conceptual critiques of a BRFSS ACE language-DIF study (Spanish vs English interview, QSTLANG), with best responses

Scope note: every critique below has the same parts: (a) the claim, (b) its strongest sources, (c) how much damage it does to THIS study (High/Medium/Low, with reasoning), and (d) the best mitigation, with recommended analytic choices and thresholds. Damage ratings and mitigation designs are my judgments, so they sit under "Inferences"; the facts they rest on sit under "Cited Findings". ## Q1. Formative vs reflective: what do Moriarity & Slavich (2025) and LaNoue & Hass (2025) actually argue, has anyone rebutted them, and what are the non-latent alternatives?

### Takeaway
Neither commentary says invariance testing of ACEs is meaningless. Moriarity & Slavich argue that a reflective common-factor model misrepresents ACEs and that invariance should instead be framed for causal-indicator models (structure, slope and residual invariance). LaNoue & Hass argue that self-reported ACEs are psychometric reports, not epidemiologic exposures, and endorse formative or latent-class models. No published rebuttal was found. The strongest response is to reframe the study as a test of item-level *reporting* equivalence. That question does not depend on whether adversity is a reflective trait, and it matters *more* under a formative or composite view, because those models assume error-free indicators. Non-latent alternatives exist, but each has a serious weakness: differential prediction is formally inconsistent with measurement invariance, and network models for binary data are mathematically equivalent to IRT.

### Cited Findings
**Exact citations and arguments**
- **Moriarity DP, Slavich GM (2025).** "Theoretical and Quantitative Disconnect When Modeling Adverse Childhood Experiences Using a Common Factor Framework: An Argument for Causal Indicator Models in Stressor Research." *Child Development* 96(4):1255–1259. doi:10.1111/cdev.14230. This is a "yes, and" commentary on Jensen, Bernard & Lanier (2024). — [Moriarity & Slavich 2025, PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12208002/)
  - Core claim: a common-factor model assumes "latent adversity" *causes* the endorsed ACEs and assumes local independence. Instead, ACEs are "organic events stemming from a child's environment," and correlations among them can arise from shared environment, which violates local independence. — [Moriarity & Slavich 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/cdev.14230)
  - They cite Rhemtulla et al. (2020): fitting a common-factor model to data generated by a formative MIMIC model produced "meaningful and unpredictable bias." — [Moriarity & Slavich 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12208002/)
  - They explicitly do *not* reject aggregation or invariance: "This is not to say that we believe ACEs aggregation through measurement models is inappropriate." Invariance "take[s] a different form for causal indicator models." Following Diamantopoulos & Papadopoulos (2010), they list three levels: **structure invariance** (same pattern of nonzero indicator weights), **slope invariance** (equal contribution of each indicator), and **residual invariance** (equal disturbance variance). Their worked example of slope noninvariance: "Divorce contributed more to adversity for Race A than Race B." — [Moriarity & Slavich 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12208002/)
  - They concede practical problems: causal-indicator models are under-identified on their own, so two reflective outcome indicators must be added (a MIMIC model). That identification approach has itself been criticized because estimates depend on which outcomes are chosen (they cite Bollen & Diamantopoulos 2017 and Rhemtulla et al. 2015). They also note a "meaningful dearth in tools" for formative invariance testing. — [Moriarity & Slavich 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12208002/)
- **LaNoue MD, Hass RW (2025; online Dec 2024).** "Conceptualizing self-reported adverse childhood experiences: From epidemiologic exposure to psychometric latent variable." *Social Science & Medicine* 366:117664. doi:10.1016/j.socscimed.2024.117664. Main points from the abstract and highlights:
  - "srACEs cannot be both exposures and indicators of scales."
  - Poor agreement between objective and subjective reports means "the epidemiologic paradigm is not a good fit."
  - They highlight the "mismatch and limitations of the reflexive measurement model" and endorse "either a formative latent variable model or application of latent class analyses."
  - They recommend formally testing competing measurement models.
  - Highlight: "srACEs assessments are not appropriate unless they are renamed 'Perceptions of ACEs Scale'."
  — [LaNoue & Hass 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.socscimed.2024.117664)
- **The target article that both commentaries respond to.** Jensen, Bernard & Lanier (2024), *Child Dev* 95(3):e170–e185, used NSCH 2018–2020 (N=93,759; 12,672 Hispanic). Methods: WLSMV on polychorics, 8 binary items plus 1 ordinal item, single factor.
  - Configural and full metric invariance held. Full scalar invariance failed (ΔCFI = −.057).
  - Under ΔCFI ≤ .002, only 3 of 11 thresholds were invariant (60% of parameters invariant, below the 80% "sufficient partial invariance" rule attributed to Dimitrov 2010).
  - Under ΔCFI ≤ .01, 16 of 20 parameters (80%) were invariant, so "sufficient partial invariance was narrowly supported."
  - Language of survey was not examined. Missing data were handled by pairwise deletion.
  — [Jensen et al. 2024, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11023799/)

**Rebuttals and follow-ups (2025–2026)**
- OpenAlex (queried 2026-11-01) lists only one work citing Moriarity & Slavich: Rohrer & Paulewicz (2025), "Rethinking measurement invariance causally." This paper reframes the debate rather than rebutting it. It argues that standard invariance procedures "rest on strong causal assumptions about the data-generating process." It says violations "imply that there are potentially interesting differences in the measurement process," and that researchers "ambivalent about latent factor models" can report sum-score and item-score group differences "as interesting descriptive findings" followed by discussion of alternative explanations. — [Rohrer & Paulewicz 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cresp.2025.100241); citing-works query via [OpenAlex](https://api.openalex.org/works?filter=cites:W4409605645)
- None of the 11 works citing LaNoue & Hass (2025–2026) is a rebuttal by title or abstract. Examples: Zhang & Lippert 2026 (SSM) on problem drinking; Weems & Zarling 2026 comparing ACE assessment approaches; a 2026 SSM "topology of adversities" paper using multiple correspondence analysis on 13 YRBS ACEs. — [OpenAlex citing list](https://api.openalex.org/works?filter=cites:W4405747351); [topology paper](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.socscimed.2026.119429)
- The closest *empirical* counterpoint is Loncar et al. (2026, J Trauma Stress 39:478–491). They fitted both reflective and composite–formative SEMs to the ACE-Q in two UK samples. Associations with outcomes were almost identical: composite–formative β Depression = .50, Anxiety = .43, Stress = .42; reflective β Depression = .51, Anxiety = .43, Stress = .42. — [Loncar et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1002/jts.70062)
- Pollak & Smith (2026, Annual Review of Clinical Psychology) push further *away* from event-count measurement toward how children "perceive, interpret, and make meaning" of their circumstances (the "Topological Approach to Adversity and Development"). This is a different line of critique: event checklists may be the wrong unit altogether. — [Pollak & Smith 2026](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-clinpsy-061724-091000)
- No reply from Jensen et al. to Moriarity & Slavich was found among works citing Jensen 2024. — [OpenAlex citing list for Jensen 2024](https://api.openalex.org/works?filter=cites:W4389245174)

**Broader causal/composite-indicator literature**
- Bollen & Bauldry (2011) argue the reflective/formative dichotomy is "too simple." They distinguish three types: **causal indicators** (conceptual unity; structural effects), **composite indicators** ("exact linear combinations of variables that need not share a concept... composites are a matter of convenience"), and **covariates**. — [Bollen & Bauldry 2011](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0024448)
- Bollen & Diamantopoulos (2017) list seven common criticisms of causal-formative indicators. One is that "they are assumed to be error-free, which is unrealistic." They argue many critiques confuse causal-formative with composite-formative indicators. — [Bollen & Diamantopoulos 2017](https://doi.org/10.1037/met0000056)
- Critics of formative measurement: Howell, Breivik & Wilcox (2007) cite interpretational confounding and conclude researchers "should opt for reflective measurement" whenever possible. Edwards (2011) calls the viability of formative measurement "a fallacy" and argues its goals "can be achieved using alternative models with reflective measures." — [Howell et al. 2007](https://doi.org/10.1037/1082-989x.12.2.205); [Edwards 2011](https://doi.org/10.1177/1094428110378369)
- Rhemtulla, van Bork & Borsboom (2020): when a common-factor model is misused, structural estimates "can be severely biased... even when model fit is perfect"; composites sometimes perform better. — [Rhemtulla et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1037/met0000220)
- Evans, Li & Whipple (2013): cumulative-risk indices designate risk arbitrarily, discard intensity, and preclude interactions. However, they "are parsimonious, are statistically sensitive even with small samples, and make no assumptions about the relative strength" of risks. — [Evans et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0031808)
- McLaughlin & Sheridan (2016): a cumulative score "fails to distinguish between distinct types of environmental experience." — [McLaughlin & Sheridan 2016](https://doi.org/10.1177/0963721416655883)
- **Empirical covariance structure of BRFSS ACE items.** Ford et al. (2014; BRFSS 2009–2010, WLSMV, weights/strata/PSU) found three correlated factors: Household Dysfunction, Emotional/Physical Abuse, Sexual Abuse. Alphas were 0.61–0.80. — [Ford et al. 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4587306/)
- Mei et al. (2022; BRFSS 2019, N=97,314, 16 states) fitted a second-order model with three subscales and reported metric and scalar invariance across age, race, sex, income, gender identity and sexual orientation. Language was not tested. — [Mei et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9706892/)
- In HCHS/SOL, "prison and divorce [items] did not cluster with the others." — [Llabre et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5285322/)
- Diamantopoulos & Papadopoulos (2010) provide the formative-invariance framework (J Int Bus Stud 41:360–370). — [Diamantopoulos & Papadopoulos 2010](https://doi.org/10.1057/jibs.2009.37)

**Non-latent alternatives and their limits**
- *Differential prediction (Cleary 1968).* Test bias is defined as group differences in the regression of a criterion on test score. — [Cleary 1968](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1745-3984.1968.tb00613.x)
  - But measurement invariance "is generally inconsistent with selection invariance." When latent means differ, sensitivity, specificity and predictive values differ across groups even under perfect measurement invariance. This is a Simpson's-paradox effect from dichotomizing scores. — [Borsboom, Romeijn & Wicherts 2008](https://doi.org/10.1037/1082-989x.13.2.75)
  - Millsap (1997) earlier showed the same tension between measurement and predictive invariance in the single-factor case. — [Millsap 1997](https://doi.org/10.1037/1082-989x.2.3.248)
- *Network invariance.* The Network Comparison Test compares network structure, edge strength and global strength by permutation. — [van Borkulo et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1037/met0000476)
  - However, the Ising model for binary items is formally related to (and statistically equivalent to) IRT models. — [Marsman et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1080/00273171.2017.1379379)
- *Anchoring vignettes* measure "response category incomparability" with hypothetical vignettes rated on the same scale and correct for it. They were designed for subjective ordinal self-assessments. — [King et al. 2004](https://doi.org/10.1017/S000305540400108X)
- *External validation against prospective records* (Baldwin et al. 2019 meta-analysis; 16 studies, N=25,471):
  - Agreement between prospective and retrospective maltreatment measures was κ = 0.19 (95% CI 0.14–0.24).
  - 52% of people with prospective records did not report maltreatment retrospectively; 56% of retrospective reporters lacked prospective records.
  - Agreement was higher with interviews than questionnaires.
  — [Baldwin et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6551848/)
- *Definition of invariance.* Invariance in the Mellenbergh/Meredith sense is defined conditional on the construct: item response distributions given the latent variable do not depend on group. — [Mellenbergh 1989](https://doi.org/10.1016/0883-0355(89)90002-5); [Meredith 1993](https://doi.org/10.1007/bf02294825)

### Inferences
**Critique 1A — "ACEs are formative or causal indicators, so a reflective DIF/invariance test is conceptually invalid."**
- *Strongest sources:* Moriarity & Slavich 2025; LaNoue & Hass 2025; Rhemtulla et al. 2020; McLaughlin & Sheridan 2016.
- *Damage: MEDIUM, rising to HIGH if the research question stays worded as "same underlying childhood adversity."* That wording assumes a latent trait of adversity, which is exactly what is under attack. The critique does *not* destroy the study, because:
  1. Neither commentary says equivalence testing is pointless. Moriarity & Slavich propose formative invariance types, and their own example of slope noninvariance is a divorce item differing by group.
  2. The quantity of interest is reporting equivalence: does a "yes" mean the same thing in both languages? That is a property of the *report*, not of the ontology of adversity.
  3. Composite and formative models assume indicators are error-free (Bollen & Diamantopoulos 2017, criticism d). Differential reporting by language therefore biases a composite ACE count directly, with no error term to absorb it. This makes equivalence more important under a formative view, not less.
- *Best response and recommended choices:*
  - (i) **Reword the estimand.** Say "conditional on the respondent's standing on the other ACE items (a reporting-relevant common dimension), is endorsement of item j independent of interview language?" Use Mellenbergh's conditional definition.
  - (ii) **Justify a common dimension empirically and theoretically.** Empirically, the ACE items have stable multi-factor structure in BRFSS (Ford 2014; Mei 2022). Theoretically, a plausible *reflective* common cause of the reports is a shared disclosure or recall propensity, alongside environmental clustering of household dysfunction. Acknowledge that model fit cannot prove reflectivity (Rhemtulla 2020).
  - (iii) **Add a formative sensitivity analysis.** Fit a MIMIC/causal-indicator model with two theoretically chosen reflective outcomes (for example, frequent mental distress and depression diagnosis). Test slope invariance of item weights by language (Diamantopoulos & Papadopoulos 2010). Report that the outcome choice affects identification.
  - (iv) **Report composite vs reflective results side by side**, following Loncar 2026, who found nearly identical associations.
  - (v) **Also report descriptive covariate-adjusted item prevalence differences**, as Rohrer & Paulewicz 2025 recommend for researchers ambivalent about latent models.

**Critique 1B — "Differential prediction is the right non-latent test of equivalent reporting."**
- *Damage if adopted naively: HIGH.* Borsboom et al. 2008 and Millsap 1997 show predictive and measurement invariance generally conflict when group means differ, so differential prediction cannot diagnose measurement bias. Also, the obvious BRFSS outcomes carry their own language DIF. Self-rated health "fair" is translated as "regular" (Viruell-Fuentes 2011; see Q4).
- *Response:* Use differential prediction only as a *secondary* consequential-validity check, with outcomes that are less language-sensitive (for example, diagnosed conditions or smoking), and never as a substitute for item-level DIF.

**Critique 1C — "Network models avoid the latent-variable assumption."**
- *Damage: LOW as an objection to the study.* The response is that the binary Ising network is statistically equivalent to IRT (Marsman 2018). Network invariance (the NCT) is therefore a reparameterization, not an escape. It can still be offered as a robustness display.

**Critique 1D — "Self-reported ACEs are perceptions, not exposures" (LaNoue & Hass).**
- *Damage: LOW to the DIF study and arguably SUPPORTIVE.* If ACE responses are reports shaped by perception, recall and disclosure, then testing whether interview language changes those reports is exactly the right question.
- *Response:* Cite LaNoue & Hass as motivation, not as a threat. Frame the study's object as "reported ACEs."

### Gaps
- The LaNoue & Hass full text was not accessible: Elsevier paywall, and the TDM API requires a key. Their specific treatment of invariance or DIF, the specific formative models they review, and the exact agreement statistics they cite could not be verified beyond the abstract and highlights.
- No published formal rebuttal of either commentary was found as of 2026-11-01 (OpenAlex citing-works check). Absence of evidence in indexing could lag for very recent items.
- I found no applied example of formative (Diamantopoulos–Papadopoulos) invariance testing of ACE items.

## Q2. The anchor / pervasive-DIF problem: if Spanish-interview respondents under-report on ALL items uniformly, can DIF methods detect it, and which designs can separate true lower exposure (immigrant paradox) from uniform under-reporting?

### Takeaway
No, not with internal data. DIF is identified only *relative* to anchor items or to an identifying assumption about the latent distribution. Uniform DIF across all items is mathematically indistinguishable from a lower latent mean. Every anchor-free method (alignment, lasso/regularized DIF, robust scaling, Gini anchor point, the minimal-L1 condition) works by assuming DIF is sparse or minimal. This is the single most damaging critique. The study can detect *item-specific* (relative) reporting differences only. The real-vs-artifact question about lower Spanish-interview ACE prevalence needs external information: a different informant or mode, prospective data, experiments, or vignettes. Encouragingly, the only BRFSS language comparison found (Alhalel 2018) shows *mixed-direction* differences, with physical abuse higher in the Spanish group. That pattern is inconsistent with purely uniform under-reporting.

### Cited Findings
- **Identification.** "The difficulty of an item is not identified from the observations, whereas the relative difficulties are." This is why DIF is reframed as differential item *pair* functioning. — [Bechger & Maris 2015](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11336-014-9408-y)
- "DIF analysis is confounded by the group difference in the latent trait distributions." Anchor items "are used to identify the latent trait distributions." Purification and regularization methods "are not guaranteed to provide valid statistical inference." Chen et al. propose a "minimal L1 norm condition for identifying the latent trait distributions." — [Chen, Li, Ouyang & Xu 2023](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11336-023-09930-9)
- Robust scaling reframes DIF "as a problem of outlier detection in IRT-based scaling." Outlier detection implies DIF items are a minority. — [Halpin 2024](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11336-024-09957-6)
- The Gini-index anchor point shifts item parameters "to an optimal position where the item parameter estimates of the groups best overlap." The criterion plot "may help discover unaccounted DIF-inducing multidimensionality." — [Strobl et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146621621990743)
- Alignment estimates group factor means "without requiring exact measurement invariance," starting from the configural model. — [Asparouhov & Muthén 2014](https://doi.org/10.1080/10705511.2014.919210)
  - Pokropek et al. (2019) recommend alignment "where there are only a few noninvariant parameters." — [Pokropek et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1080/10705511.2018.1561293)
- Iterative one-item-at-a-time procedures "can fail to select the correct anchor items and identify true DIF, particularly when DIF is present in many items." Belzak & Bauer propose a lasso penalty on group differences in 2PL item parameters. — [Belzak & Bauer 2020](https://doi.org/10.1037/met0000253)
- Anchor selection "is crucial"; new strategies "can reliably locate a suitable anchor when the sample sizes are large enough." — [Kopf, Zeileis & Strobl 2015](https://doi.org/10.1177/0013164414529792)
- "Group-invariant anchors were more difficult to identify as the percentage of differentially functioning items increased." — [Woods 2009](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146621607314044)
- ETS review: refining the matching criterion "improves detection rates when DIF is primarily in one direction but can depress detection rates when DIF is balanced." — [Zwick 2012](https://doi.org/10.1002/j.2333-8504.2012.tb02290.x)
- "There is unavoidable ambiguity in how researchers can define comparisons if measurement invariance is violated." There is "no support for preferring the partial invariance approach over competing approaches, such as invariance alignment, robust linking, or Bayesian approximate invariance." — [Robitzsch & Lüdtke 2023](https://doi.org/10.1080/10705511.2023.2191292)
- Bayesian approximate invariance replaces exact-zero constraints with small-variance priors. — [Muthén & Asparouhov 2012](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0026802); [van de Schoot et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2013.00770)
- **Direct BRFSS evidence by questionnaire language (grey literature).** Alhalel (2018, Northwestern MPH culminating thesis) analyzed BRFSS 2011–2012 from 9 states (IA, MN, MT, NC, OK, TN, VT, WA, WI): 2,983 Hispanic respondents, 61% English-preference and about 39% Spanish-preference, with complex survey commands.
  - 6 of 11 items differed (p<0.01).
  - English-preference respondents more often reported being touched sexually, household substance abuse, household incarceration, household mental illness, and parental separation/divorce.
  - Spanish-preference respondents were "almost twice as likely" to report physical abuse: **28.8% vs 15.9%**.
  - Spanish-preference respondents were younger, more often male, less educated, lower income and uninsured.
  — [Alhalel 2018 thesis, Northwestern PRISM](https://prism.northwestern.edu/records/7mt6a-brh38)
- **Immigrant-paradox evidence from other informants and designs:**
  - NSCH 2011–12 (parent report), 12,162 Hispanic children: 2+ ACEs in 16% of immigrant-family vs 30% of US-native-family children; adjusted OR 0.46 (0.34–0.61) despite more poverty (80% vs 47% ≤200% FPL). The authors caution: "This may not reflect a true health advantage... or ACE questions may not capture the adverse experiences specific to immigrant families." — [Caballero et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2017-0297)
  - NSCH low-income Latino children: 2+ ACEs in 13% of immigrant vs 40% of nonimmigrant children. — [Loria & Caughy 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jpeds.2017.09.056)
  - NESARC: "With the exception of neglect, the prevalence of ACE was markedly higher among native-born Americans and second-generation immigrants compared with first-generation immigrants." — [Vaughn et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1177/0886260515589568)
  - HCHS/SOL: 4+ ACEs in 37.6% of US-born vs 26.3% of those born outside the US mainland; Cuban heritage lowest at 20.2%. — [Llabre et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5285322/)
  - Prospective parenting behavior: foreign-born Hispanic mothers and fathers "used less spanking toward their young children" than US-born Hispanic parents, controlling for confounds (n=1,089 mothers, 650 fathers). — [Lee & Altschul 2015](https://doi.org/10.1177/0886260514535098)
- **Retrospective error structure.** Retrospective reports "involve a substantial rate of false negatives, and substantial measurement error... false positive reports are probably rare." — [Hardt & Rutter 2004](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1469-7610.2004.00218.x)
- **Language DIF can be large.** In the MMSE, 12 of 21 items showed uniform language DIF, and "DIF accounted for two-thirds of the observed difference" in cognitive level between Spanish- and English-administered groups (MIMIC). — [Jones 2006](https://doi.org/10.1097/01.mlr.0000245250.50114.0f)
  - In the Latino National Survey, "language of interview systematically colors Latinos' interpretation of survey items," shown with MIMIC. — [Pérez 2011](https://doi.org/10.1093/pan/mpr029)

### Inferences
**Critique 2A — "Your DIF analysis cannot detect the bias that matters: uniform under-reporting of all ACEs in Spanish interviews."**
- *Strongest sources:* Bechger & Maris 2015; Chen et al. 2023; Robitzsch & Lüdtke 2023; Woods 2009; Zwick 2012.
- *Damage: HIGH.* The policy-relevant question is whether lower Spanish-language ACE prevalence is real. Item-level DIF methods are structurally blind to a constant shift on all items, because that shift is absorbed into the latent mean. They are biased when most items shift in one direction, because anchors become contaminated. A null DIF result therefore cannot be read as "equivalent reporting." It can only mean "no item is out of line with the others."
- *Best response:*
  - (i) **State the estimand honestly:** relative (item-specific) DIF.
  - (ii) **Show sensitivity to anchors.** Re-estimate the language difference in latent mean or expected ACE count under several defensible identification schemes: all-other-items purified anchors; lasso (Belzak & Bauer 2020); robust scaling (Halpin 2024); alignment; Bayesian approximate invariance (prior variance 0.01–0.05 is commonly used, though that value range is not verified here). Present the range, as Robitzsch & Lüdtke 2023 recommend acknowledging ambiguity.
  - (iii) **Choose theory-based anchors a priori.** Use items least plausibly affected by language or stigma. Pre-register them and test their stability.
  - (iv) **Test a "sensitivity gradient" prediction.** If language-linked *disclosure* drives under-reporting, DIF against Spanish should grow with item sensitivity (sexual abuse > household mental illness > substance use > divorce). If a cultural *threshold* effect drives it, the pattern should be item-specific (physical abuse). This is a falsifiable pattern prediction that internal data *can* test.
  - (v) **Triangulate externally.** Other informants and designs show the same direction of the immigrant/nativity gap: parent report in NSCH (Caballero 2017; Loria 2018), in-person HCHS/SOL (Llabre 2017), NESARC (Vaughn 2017), and *contemporaneous* parenting behavior (Lee & Altschul 2015). That consistency makes "entirely artifactual" less plausible, though not impossible, since all are self- or parent-reports.
  - (vi) **Propose design extensions** that can identify uniform bias (see 2B).

**Critique 2B — "Only external information can break the identification problem."** Designs that can, ranked by feasibility:
1. **Mode, informant and design triangulation** with existing data, as above. Feasible now.
2. **Within-Hispanic decomposition by covariates (MNLFA).** Ask whether language DIF persists after allowing DIF by education, age and income (Bauer 2017; see Q3). This does not identify uniform bias but narrows interpretation.
3. **Embedded randomized experiments in future data collection.** Randomly assign interview language among bilingual respondents. Use split-ballot item wording, for example the Spanish physical-abuse item with versus without "un padre/madre" or "nalgadas", or alternative verbs to "pegó". This mirrors question-context experiments that changed Spanish-speakers' self-rated health (Lee & Schwarz 2014; Lee, Schwarz & Goldstein 2014; see Q4).
4. **Anchoring vignettes** (King 2004) for threshold-type items, such as physical and verbal abuse ("Would you call this 'le pegó / lastimó físicamente'?"). This targets norm-driven threshold differences, not recall.
5. **Record linkage or prospective cohorts.** Gold standard but impractical for BRFSS. Baldwin 2019 shows retrospective-prospective agreement is poor overall (κ=0.19), so even this "gold standard" is noisy.

**Critique 2C — "Lower prevalence is just the immigrant paradox; there is no DIF to find."**
- *Damage: LOW to MEDIUM.* The Alhalel BRFSS data show *opposite-direction* item differences: physical abuse 28.8% Spanish vs 15.9% English, while five other items were higher in English. Mixed-direction, item-specific differences are exactly what relative DIF methods can detect, and they cannot be explained by a uniform exposure difference alone.
- *Caveat:* Alhalel's comparison is marginal (unconditional) and bivariate, from 9 low-Hispanic-density states, and is an unpublished thesis. It shows heterogeneity of item differences, not DIF per se.

### Gaps
- I found no study that empirically separated uniform under-reporting from true exposure differences for ACEs by language, through experiments, vignettes or record linkage.
- Camilli's classic chapter on the limits of internal-criterion DIF ("The case against item bias detection techniques based on internal criteria," in Holland & Wainer, *Differential Item Functioning*, 1993) could not be verified online; Crossref does not index it.
- Pérez & Tavits (2017, AJPS, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1111/ajps.12290)) is often cited as a randomized interview-language design among bilinguals. Its abstract and design were not retrieved, so the claim is unverified here.
- The Alhalel thesis is non-peer-reviewed. Table-level item prevalences other than physical abuse were not extractable from the text layer.
- Recommended prior variances for Bayesian approximate invariance with binary items were not verified from primary sources in this pass.

## Q3. DIF methods for binary/ordinal items in a large complex survey sample: over-powered tests, weak fit-change rules, effect sizes and thresholds, weights/strata/PSUs, multiple testing, focal-group size, sparse items

### Takeaway
Do not rely on χ² difference tests (meaningless at N≈100k) or on ΔCFI. ΔCFI is attenuated by unequal group sizes, and the Spanish group is about 4–8% of the sample. ΔCFI also performs poorly with WLSMV under misspecification, and published ACE studies flipped conclusions with the cutoff (Jensen 2024). Use item-level, model-based DIF with effect sizes and impact measures: IRT/MIMIC/MNLFA, logistic regression DIF, Mantel–Haenszel, dMACS, and DTF/DRF. Add equivalence testing, Benjamini–Hochberg control, design-based variance, and special handling for sparse sexual-abuse and incarceration items.

### Cited Findings
**Global fit-change rules**
- Cheung & Rensvold (2002) recommend ΔCFI, ΔGamma-hat and ΔMcDonald's NCI, stating these "are independent of both model complexity and sample size." The conventional cutoff is ΔCFI ≤ −.01. — [Cheung & Rensvold 2002](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15328007sem0902_5)
- Chen (2007): changes in fit "are affected by the interaction between the pattern of invariance and the proportion of invariant items." The relation is "nonmonotonic" when noninvariance is uniform. "**Changes are bigger when sample sizes are equal rather than when they are unequal.**" — [Chen 2007](https://doi.org/10.1080/10705510701301834)
- Meade, Johnson & Braddy (2008) suggested a general ΔCFI cutoff of .002 and condition-specific ΔNCI. — [Meade et al. 2008](https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.93.3.568)
- Sass, Schmitt & Marsh (2014; 342 conditions, ordered categorical data): "researchers who use the ΔAFI with WLSMV should use caution, as these statistics do not perform well with misspecified models." Δχ² power and Type I error varied with estimator. — [Sass et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1080/10705511.2014.882658)
- For categorical indicators with large groups (n=600–6000), Rutkowski & Svetina (2017) suggested ΔRMSEA ≤ 0.01 together with ΔCFI ≥ −0.004 for equal-threshold and equal-loading invariance (as summarized in a 2024 application). — [Rutkowski & Svetina 2017](https://doi.org/10.1080/08957347.2016.1243540); summarized in [Psychol Med 2024 application](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0033291724002447)
  - Many typical criteria are "unsuitable in a large group and varied sample-size context." — [Rutkowski & Svetina 2014](https://doi.org/10.1177/0013164413498257)
- Identification of categorical-indicator invariance models matters: conventional baseline identification "may become restrictions and define statistically non-equivalent models, leading to different conclusions." — [Wu & Estabrook 2016](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11336-016-9506-0); tutorial in [Svetina, Rutkowski & Rutkowski 2020](https://doi.org/10.1080/10705511.2019.1602776)
- The established chi-square-difference rule "is unable to control either Type I or Type II errors." — [Yuan & Chan 2016](https://doi.org/10.1037/met0000080)
  - Equivalence testing (EQ) "was the only procedure that maintained empirical error rates below the nominal alpha" but "requires larger sample sizes." — [Counsell, Cribbie & Flora 2020](https://doi.org/10.1080/00273171.2019.1633617)
- **ACE precedents built on ΔCFI:**
  - Jensen 2024 (NSCH): the conclusion flipped between ΔCFI ≤ .002 (insufficient partial invariance) and ≤ .01 (sufficient). — [Jensen et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11023799/)
  - Mei 2022 (BRFSS 2019; WLSMV, theta parameterization, FIML): concluded the BRFSS ACE scale "is equitable and free from measurement bias" across race and other groups based on omnibus ΔCFI/ΔTLI. The table excerpts reviewed show step-wise ΔCFI values of roughly .000–.008 (for example, age scalar step ΔCFI .003). — [Mei et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9706892/)

**Item-level methods and effect-size thresholds**
- *Logistic and ordinal logistic regression DIF:*
  - Zumbo's pseudo-R² guidelines: negligible < 0.13, moderate 0.13–0.26, large > 0.26.
  - Crane et al. proposed a 10% change in the ability coefficient (β1) as practically meaningful uniform DIF; later studies used 5% and 1%.
  - In lordif simulations, "a 10% change in β1 is roughly equivalent to 0.02 in Nagelkerke's R²."
  — [Choi, Gibbons & Crane 2011 (lordif), PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3093114/)
  - The logistic regression procedure "tends to produce inflated Type I errors." Adding an effect-size criterion "can substantially reduce Type I error rates when large sample sizes" are used. — [Jodoin & Gierl 2001](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15324818ame1404_2)
  - Crane et al. (2006) applied OLR-DIF (difwithpar) including language of administration on the MMSE, with iterative IRT ability purification. — [Crane et al. 2006](https://doi.org/10.1097/01.mlr.0000245183.28384.ed)
- *Mantel–Haenszel and ETS classification:* "The ETS C rule often displays low DIF detection rates even when samples are large." — [Zwick 2012](https://doi.org/10.1002/j.2333-8504.2012.tb02290.x); see also [Holland & Thayer 1986](https://doi.org/10.1002/j.2330-8516.1986.tb00186.x); [Rogers & Swaminathan 1993](https://doi.org/10.1177/014662169301700201)
- *MIMIC:* effective with 2PL data but with "a very high rate of incorrect DIF identification for 20 items with three-parameter logistic data." — [Finch 2005](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146621605275728)
  - Nonuniform DIF through latent interaction (LMS) gave "severely inflated" Type I error with small focal groups. — [Woods & Grimm 2011](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146621611405984)
- *MNLFA* lets item parameters vary with multiple categorical *and* continuous covariates at once (for example, language, age, education). — [Bauer 2017](https://doi.org/10.1037/met0000077)
- *dMACS:* proposed for CFA equivalence and linked to group mean differences and adverse impact. — [Nye & Drasgow 2011](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0022955)
  - Tentative benchmarks: 0.20–0.40 small, 0.40–0.70 medium, ≥0.70 large (Nye et al. 2019, as summarized in a 2024 application). — [Nye et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1177/1094428118761122); summarized in [Psychol Med 2024](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0033291724002447)
  - Six effect sizes for continuous outcomes were evaluated; three were unbiased in all conditions. — [Gunn, Grimm & Edwards 2020](https://doi.org/10.1080/10705511.2019.1689507)
  - IRT-based taxonomy of item and scale effect sizes. — [Meade 2010](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0018966); [Steinberg & Thissen 2006](https://doi.org/10.1037/1082-989x.11.4.402)
- *Differential test functioning:* improved DTF statistics that account for sampling variability, with consistent Type I error for dichotomous and polytomous IRT. — [Chalmers, Counsell & Flora 2016](https://doi.org/10.1177/0013164415584576)
  - Compensatory and non-compensatory differential response functioning (DRF) for item bundles. — [Chalmers 2018](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11336-018-9626-9)
- *RMSD item-fit:* a group-specific item-fit statistic used for invariance across language groups in PISA 2015. — [Buchholz & Hartig 2019](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146621617748323)

**Sample size, multiple testing and complex design**
- *Focal-group size.* Ordinal LR DIF with short scales: "moderate uniform DIF in a two-item scale required a sample size of 300 per group for adequate (>80%) power. For longer scales, a sample size of 200 was adequate." "Considerably larger sample sizes were required to detect nonuniform DIF, when there were extreme [floor effects] or when a reduced type I error rate was required." — [Scott et al. 2009](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jclinepi.2008.06.003)
- *Multiple testing:* Benjamini–Hochberg FDR control for DIF across many items. — [Thissen, Steinberg & Kuang 2002](https://doi.org/10.3102/10769986027001077)
- *Complex survey design:* pseudo-ML and weighted least squares with sampling weights (including categorical factor models) under stratified cluster sampling in Mplus. — [Asparouhov 2005](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15328007sem1203_4); [Oberski 2014, lavaan.survey](https://doi.org/10.18637/jss.v057.i01)
  - Ford et al. (2014) included BRFSS weight, stratum and PSU in all ACE CFA/MGCFA analyses (Mplus, WLSMV). — [Ford et al. 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4587306/)
- *Sparse items and separation:* Firth bias-reduced likelihood and its use against separation in logistic regression. — [Firth 1993](https://doi.org/10.1093/biomet/80.1.27); [Heinze & Schemper 2002](https://doi.org/10.1002/sim.1047)

**BRFSS facts that set the feasible design**
- 2023 BRFSS codebook, QSTLANG: English 414,251 (95.60% unweighted, 91.77% weighted); **Spanish 18,397 (4.25%; 7.42% weighted)**; Other 675. — [BRFSS 2023 codebook (zip)](https://www.cdc.gov/brfss/annual_data/2023/zip/codebook23_llcp-v2-508.zip)
- In 2023 the ACE module was answered by about 55,800 respondents (for example, ACEDIVRC non-missing = 55,808). It contains **13 items**, including ACEADSAF ("adult... made you feel safe and protected") and ACEADNED ("tried hard to make sure your basic needs were met"), both on 5-point frequency scales. — [BRFSS 2023 codebook](https://www.cdc.gov/brfss/annual_data/2023/zip/codebook23_llcp-v2-508.zip)
- 2023 weighted "yes" or any-frequency prevalence of sparse items: forced sex 5.38% (1.76% once + 3.62% more than once); tried to make you touch 9.61%; touched sexually 12.26%; household incarceration 9.15%. — [BRFSS 2023 codebook](https://www.cdc.gov/brfss/annual_data/2023/zip/codebook23_llcp-v2-508.zip)
- 2023 item refusal ranged from 0.98% (incarceration) to 2.14% (touched sexually), with sexual-abuse items about 2%. "Don't know" ran 0.28–1.68%. — [BRFSS 2023 codebook](https://www.cdc.gov/brfss/annual_data/2023/zip/codebook23_llcp-v2-508.zip)

### Inferences
**Critique 3A — "At N≈100k everything is significant; global ΔCFI is insensitive and will miss item-level problems."**
- *Damage: MEDIUM, but HIGH if the study leans on MGCFA/ΔCFI.* Three reasons:
  1. Chen (2007) shows fit changes shrink with unequal group sizes, and the Spanish group is roughly 1:12 to 1:20.
  2. Large *uniform* noninvariance has a nonmonotonic relation with ΔCFI.
  3. With 11–13 items, one strongly non-invariant item barely moves global fit. Mei 2022's "free from measurement bias" conclusion on BRFSS illustrates this failure mode.
- *Best response and recommended thresholds:*
  - (i) Treat global fit as descriptive. If MGCFA is reported, use categorical-specific criteria (ΔCFI ≥ −.004 *and* ΔRMSEA ≤ .01; Rutkowski & Svetina 2017), identify per Wu & Estabrook 2016, and add equivalence tests (Yuan & Chan 2016; Counsell et al. 2020). The large N gives EQ enough power.
  - (ii) Make **item-level** inference primary. Flag DIF only if it is (a) significant after BH-FDR at q=.05 (Thissen et al. 2002) *and* (b) exceeds a magnitude threshold. Use **Nagelkerke ΔR² ≥ .02, or β1 change ≥ 5–10%** (lordif conventions) for LR DIF; **ETS category B/C** for MH; **dMACS ≥ 0.20** (small) / **≥ 0.40** (medium) (Nye et al. 2019 benchmarks); or a pre-specified expected-score difference (for example, ≥ 2–3 percentage points in endorsement probability at the median trait). The last is my suggestion and not a literature standard.
  - (iii) Always report **DTF/DRF** (Chalmers 2016, 2018) on the expected ACE count, since that is what users consume.
  - (iv) Pre-register.

**Critique 3B — "The Spanish subgroup is too small, especially for rare items, and the analysis will be underpowered or unstable."**
- *Damage: MEDIUM.*
  - With about 4% Spanish interviews nationally and about 55.8k ACE respondents in 2023, a rough *expectation* is on the order of 1,000–3,000 Spanish-interview ACE respondents per year. This is my inference: the share among ACE-module states is unknown and depends on which states field the module. Alhalel's 9-state, 2-year pool had about 1,160 Spanish-preference respondents.
  - Scott 2009's ~200–300 per group is enough for moderate *uniform* DIF on ordinal items, but floor effects are extreme here (85–95% "never" on sexual-abuse items). Nonuniform DIF and sparse items need much larger samples.
  - At a plausible 2–4% forced-sex prevalence in the Spanish group, 2,000 respondents yields only about 40–80 endorsers. Matching-score strata in MH will be empty, and logistic regression may separate.
- *Response:*
  - (i) Pool multiple years with identical wording, after checking invariance across years and module versions.
  - (ii) Prioritize uniform DIF; treat nonuniform DIF as exploratory.
  - (iii) Collapse the three sexual-abuse items into CDC's single "sexual abuse" category or model them as a testlet. They are also locally dependent.
  - (iv) Use Firth-penalized or Bayesian (weakly informative) logistic models.
  - (v) Use IRT/MNLFA with trait estimates rather than coarse sum-score strata.
  - (vi) Report minimum detectable effect from simulation calibrated to the observed prevalences.

**Critique 3C — "You ignored the complex sampling design."**
- *Damage: LOW to MEDIUM.* It is solvable. But many ACE invariance precedents (Jensen 2024; Mei 2022) do not clearly report design-based estimation, and reviewers will check.
- *Response:* Estimate with weights, strata and PSU (Mplus WLSMV/MLR with TYPE=COMPLEX, as in Ford 2014; or lavaan with sampling weights and cluster-robust SEs; or survey-weighted LR DIF). Report weighted vs unweighted sensitivity. Note that DIF concerns conditional parameters, for which weighting mainly affects SEs and representativeness of the parameter population.

**Critique 3D — "Unidimensional DIF models on a three-factor instrument manufacture spurious DIF."**
- *Damage: MEDIUM.* BRFSS ACEs are multidimensional (Ford 2014; Mei 2022). If the Spanish group differs in the *mean of a specific factor* (for example, sexual abuse) relative to the general factor, a unidimensional model flags DIF on those items. Strobl 2021 notes that DIF can signal "unaccounted DIF-inducing multidimensionality."
- *Response:* Run DIF within a correlated three-factor or bifactor IRT/MNLFA model. Check dimensionality separately by language first (configural step).

**Critique 3E — "Binary scoring discards the frequency information where language effects may live."**
- *Damage: LOW to MEDIUM.*
- *Response:* Model the six frequency items as ordinal (graded response). Category-use DIF ("once" vs "more than once") is a plausible locus of response-style effects (see Q4).

### Gaps
- Jodoin & Gierl's specific ΔR² classification cutoffs (commonly cited as < .035 negligible, .035–.070 moderate, > .070 large) could not be verified in the abstract or the lordif text retrieved here. Verify in the primary article before citing numbers.
- I found no simulation study specific to binary DIF power under BRFSS-like conditions: about 5% focal group, 11–13 items, item prevalences of 5–35%, and design effects.
- The exact ETS A/B/C cut values (|MH D-DIF| <1.0; ≥1.5) are standard, but they were not verified from Zwick 2012's full text in this pass.
- Mei 2022 and Jensen 2024 do not clearly report whether survey weights or PSUs entered the invariance models.
- The number of states and the Spanish-interview count specifically among ACE-module respondents per year was not computed. That requires microdata.

## Q4. Language-of-interview effects in US health surveys: what is documented, and does it extend to sensitive and abuse disclosure?

### Takeaway
Interview language is one of the best-documented sources of measurement non-equivalence in US Latino health data. Examples include the self-rated health "fair" = "regular" translation, question-context effects on Spanish speakers only, and DIF in the BDI, MMSE, GAD-7/HSCL-25 and political-knowledge items. Well-built translations can be nearly DIF-free (PROMIS pain interference: 1 of 41 items). Language marks acculturation, education and nativity together. Direct evidence on Spanish-language *disclosure of childhood abuse* in CATI surveys was not found. The closest analogues are higher Hispanic nonresponse to sexual-orientation questions in BRFSS, and qualitative and cultural literature on child sexual abuse disclosure.

### Cited Findings
- **Self-rated health.** Being interviewed in Spanish was associated with higher odds of fair/poor self-rated health in both the Chicago Community Adult Health Study and BRFSS 2003. Adjusting for interview language "substantially reduced the gap between Whites and Latinos." Spanish interviewees preferred "fair (regular in Spanish)." — [Viruell-Fuentes et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.2105/AJPH.2009.175455)
  - In L.A.FANS, results "underscore the importance of language of interview over other measures of acculturation, suggesting that translation issues... may give rise to some of the differences." — [Bzostek, Goldman & Pebley 2007](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.socscimed.2007.04.028)
- **BRFSS Spanish vs English Hispanics (2003–2005; 45,076 Hispanic adults, 23 states).** Fair/poor health 39% vs 17%; uninsured 55% vs 23%. Chronic disease, obesity and smoking were *lower* among Spanish speakers. "Adjustment for demographic and socioeconomic factors did not mitigate the influence of language." — [DuBard & Gizlice 2008](https://doi.org/10.2105/AJPH.2007.119008)
- **Question context.** Context "influenced SRH reports of Spanish speakers (and Hispanics) significantly but not of English speakers." Asking SRH after specific health questions reduced the Hispanic–White gap by two-thirds and improved mortality prediction consistency. — [Lee & Schwarz 2014](https://doi.org/10.2105/AJPH.2012.301055)
  - The context effect was larger among unmarried and less-educated Spanish speakers. — [Lee, Schwarz & Goldstein 2014](https://doi.org/10.1177/0898264314532688)
- **Interview language as an acculturation proxy.** Among Asian Americans in CHIS 2003, foreign-born respondents interviewed in English resembled US-born respondents more than foreign-born respondents interviewed in other languages. "Interview language may serve as a better measure for acculturation especially among foreign-born populations." — [Lee, Nguyen & Tsui 2011](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10903-009-9278-z)
- **Scale DIF by language:**
  - BDI Spanish vs English (n=292): "regardless of level of depression, Latinos are more likely to endorse items reflecting tearfulness and punishment, and less likely to endorse inability to work." — [Azocar et al. 2001](https://doi.org/10.1002/jclp.1017)
  - GAD-7/HSCL-25 in four languages: "Spanish speakers reported more worry symptoms and less somatic symptoms for reasons unrelated to their psychological distress." — [Cruz-Gonzalez et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyt.2021.579173)
  - PROMIS pain interference: only 1 of 41 items showed uniform language DIF (n=527 Spanish-speaking Latinos). — [Paz et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11136-017-1499-3)
  - MMSE: 12 of 21 items showed DIF, accounting for two-thirds of the group difference. — [Jones 2006](https://doi.org/10.1097/01.mlr.0000245250.50114.0f)
  - Latino National Survey political items: language "systematically colors Latinos' interpretation of survey items." — [Pérez 2011](https://doi.org/10.1093/pan/mpr029)
- **Response styles.** Across four datasets (Hispanics N=1,908; non-Hispanic Whites N=14,425), Hispanics showed more extreme responding and acquiescence. These "decrease as Hispanics acculturate," and less-educated respondents were more extreme. — [Marín, Gamba & Marín 1992](https://doi.org/10.1177/0022022192234006)
- **Sensitive questions.** Misreporting on sensitive topics "is quite common and... largely situational," depends on "design features of the survey," and is "a more or less motivated process" of editing answers in the presence of an interviewer. — [Tourangeau & Yan 2007](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.133.5.859)
  - In Washington BRFSS, nonresponse to the sexual-orientation question was higher among Asian Americans, Hispanics and African Americans than among non-Hispanic Whites. — [Kim & Fredriksen-Goldsen 2013](https://doi.org/10.2105/AJPH.2012.300835)
  - "Cultural norms affect the likelihood that child sexual abuse will be discovered by an adult or disclosed by a child," and whether families report it. — [Fontes & Plummer 2010](https://doi.org/10.1080/10538712.2010.512520)
  - Behaviorally specific wording affects disclosure, and traditional labels may lead to under-reporting of sexual violence in BIPOC and SGM communities (2026 review). — [Prakash et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1002/jts.70097)
- **BRFSS ACE module nonresponse (2019: 21 states; 2021: 16 states).** Module nonresponse was 1.2% and 2.4%. Nonresponders were more likely non-Hispanic Black, non-Hispanic Asian, unemployed, low income, or in poor health. Hispanic share among nonresponders vs responders: 10.0% vs 10.0% in 2019 and 12.3% vs 10.6% in 2021, not flagged as significant. "Nonresponse by question increased as the module progressed, and nonresponse was highest for sexual abuse questions." **Interview language was not analyzed.** — [Besera et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11752077/)

### Inferences
**Critique 4A — "Language effects are real, but you can't say whether they come from translation, culture, acculturation, response style or interviewer."**
- *Damage: MEDIUM.* The literature shows language DIF is common but heterogeneous in mechanism. A DIF flag alone will not say *why*.
- *Response:*
  - (i) Pair each flagged item with a pre-specified mechanism hypothesis: translation (wording differences; see Q5), threshold/norm (physical abuse), disclosure/stigma (sexual abuse, mental illness), or structural (divorce or "not married").
  - (ii) Use MNLFA to see whether DIF by language survives adjustment for DIF by education and age. Education is a known moderator of response styles and context effects (Marín 1992; Lee et al. 2014).
  - (iii) Analyze DK/refusal by language as a separate outcome. Besera 2024 did not.

**Critique 4B — "Social desirability and familismo will suppress Spanish-language disclosure of household problems, so DIF should be pervasive, which you can't detect" (links to Q2).**
- *Damage: HIGH in principle, MEDIUM given the evidence.* No direct evidence was found that Spanish CATI respondents under-disclose ACEs. The Alhalel data show *more* physical-abuse reporting in Spanish, which argues against a blanket suppression effect.
- *Response:* Use the sensitivity-gradient test (Q2) and DK/refusal analyses. Cite Tourangeau & Yan 2007: if anything, misreporting is item- and situation-specific, which favors detectability.

**Critique 4C — "Interview language mixes response styles (acquiescence or extremity) with content effects."**
- *Damage: LOW to MEDIUM* for binary yes/no items; higher for the never/once/more-than-once items.
- *Response:* Model those items as graded and test category-threshold DIF. Use binary collapsing as a robustness check that removes extremity-style effects.

### Gaps
- I found no study testing ACE (or child maltreatment) item DIF by interview language in BRFSS, NHIS, NSCH or HCHS/SOL, apart from the Alhalel marginal comparison.
- I found no study of Spanish- vs English-language *item nonresponse or break-off* in the BRFSS ACE module.
- Ross & Mirowsky (1984, JHSB, [doi](https://doi.org/10.2307/2136668)) on social desirability and acquiescence in a US–Mexico mental health survey is relevant, but its abstract was not available, so its specific findings are not reported here.
- Huang et al. 2006 on PHQ-9 across ethnic groups ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1525-1497.2006.00409.x)) was identified, but its specific DIF results were not retrieved.

## Q5. Construct and content validity of specific ACE items across cultures, including the actual BRFSS Spanish wording

### Takeaway
A side-by-side reading of the CDC English and Spanish ACE modules shows several non-trivial wording differences that make item-specific DIF *a priori* plausible. The physical-abuse item drops "parent" and uses "pegó," a verb commonly used for disciplinary hitting, while excluding only "nalgadas." The verbal-abuse item uses "le humilló" (humiliated) for "put you down." The divorce item offers "Los padres no estaban casados." Culturally, corporal punishment is more normative in Latino families, cohabitation is widespread in Latin America, migration creates parent–child separations the item does not capture, and incarceration in countries of origin can be political. All of this supports the study's motivation, and it also means DIF may reflect *real conditional differences* rather than "bias."

### Cited Findings
- **CDC module wording (2009–2020 module PDFs).** — [CDC English module](https://www.cdc.gov/violenceprevention/pdf/BRFSS_Adverse_Module.pdf); [CDC Spanish module](https://www.cdc.gov/violenceprevention/pdf/BRFSS_Adverse_Module_Spanish.pdf)
  - *Physical abuse.* English: "Before age 18, how often did **a parent or adult** in your home ever hit, beat, kick, or physically hurt you in any way? Do not include spanking." Spanish: "Antes de que usted cumpliera 18 años, ¿con qué frecuencia **un adulto en su hogar le pegó, golpeó, pateó o lastimó físicamente** de alguna otra forma? No incluya las nalgadas."
  - *Verbal abuse.* English: "swear at you, insult you, or put you down." Spanish: "le dijo groserías, le insultó o **le humilló**."
  - *Separation/divorce.* English: "Were your parents separated or divorced?" with options Yes / No / 8 = Parents not married / DK / Refused. Spanish: "¿Sus padres estaban separados o divorciados?" with option 8 = "Los padres no estaban casados." The Spanish PDF prints this item twice, with codes 1/2 and 3/4, a document error.
  - *Witnessed IPV.* Spanish: "se dieron bofetadas, patadas, puñetazos o golpes." English: "slap, hit, kick, punch or beat each other up."
- **2023 production wording and distribution.** The 2023 physical-abuse item reads "Not including spanking, (before age 18), how often did a parent or adult in your home ever hit, beat, kick, or physically hurt you in any way?"
  - Separation/divorce 2023: Yes 30.79% weighted; "Parents not married" 1.72% unweighted / 2.37% weighted.
  — [BRFSS 2023 codebook](https://www.cdc.gov/brfss/annual_data/2023/zip/codebook23_llcp-v2-508.zip)
- **CDC ACE page notes:** the abuse questions were modified from the original CDC-Kaiser study for telephone administration; the original IPV item covered only the mother; emotional and physical neglect items were added (2019 per the page). — [CDC BRFSS ACE page](https://www.cdc.gov/violenceprevention/aces/ace-brfss.html)
- **Corporal punishment norms.** In a national opt-in panel of parents of children under 5, "Spanking was most frequent among Latinos (73%) and lowest among White parents (59%)." — [Klevens et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10560-018-0591-z)
  - Foreign-born Hispanic parents spanked *less* than US-born Hispanic parents. — [Lee & Altschul 2015](https://doi.org/10.1177/0886260514535098)
- **Observed language difference on physical abuse.** In BRFSS 2011–12 Hispanics, Spanish-preference respondents reported physical abuse at 28.8% vs 15.9% for English-preference respondents. — [Alhalel 2018](https://prism.northwestern.edu/records/7mt6a-brh38)
- **Incarceration and divorce items in Latino samples.** In HCHS/SOL, "prison and divorce did not cluster with the others." "The political situation in the country of origin and the immigration experience may have led to having a parent go to prison." — [Llabre et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5285322/)
- **Family structure.** Latin America experienced a "cohabitation boom" between 1970 and 2007. — [Esteve, Lesthaeghe & López-Gay 2012](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1728-4457.2012.00472.x)
  - Immigrant families commonly experience parent–child separation and later reunification during migration. — [Suárez-Orozco, Todorova & Louie 2002](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1545-5300.2002.00625.x)
- **Construct under-representation for immigrant families.** "ACE questions may not capture the adverse experiences specific to immigrant families." — [Caballero et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2017-0297)
  - A 2026 pilot in Ecuador (n=93; both instruments in Spanish) compared ACE-10 with a modified ACE-14. Mean counts were similar (1.23 vs 1.25), but Bland–Altman limits for individual differences were about ±2 domains. "Deprivation or impaired caregiving showed the clearest directional difference." — [Ecuador pilot 2026](https://doi.org/10.3390/bs16111982)
- **Dimensionality.** In NSCH under 0–5 age stratification, invariance was much weaker; only one loading was invariant across racial/ethnic groups. — [Jensen et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11023799/)

### Inferences
**Critique 5A — "DIF on divorce, physical abuse or incarceration reflects real conditional differences in family life and childhood context, not reporting bias, so calling it 'bias' is wrong."**
- *Damage: HIGH for interpretation, LOW for feasibility.* For example, two people with the same overall adversity may differ in parental divorce because a parent in a non-marital union who separates may be recorded as "not married," and because divorce is less common where cohabitation predominates. That is DIF by definition but not a *reporting* error. It is exactly Moriarity & Slavich's "slope noninvariance" and Rohrer & Paulewicz's "substantively meaningful violation."
- *Response:*
  - (i) Use the vocabulary of "non-equivalence," not "bias."
  - (ii) Classify each item a priori as *reporting-sensitive* (abuse, household mental illness, substance use) vs *structurally-variable* (divorce, incarceration). Interpret DIF differently for each class.
  - (iii) Handle the "Parents not married" code explicitly. Show results with it coded as 0, coded as missing, and with it as a separate category. Test whether the "not married" share differs by language.
  - (iv) Treat the separation item as a likely *non-anchor*.

**Critique 5B — "The Spanish physical-abuse wording is not equivalent: 'un adulto... le pegó' vs 'a parent or adult... hit', with only 'nalgadas' excluded."**
- *Damage: MEDIUM. This supports the study but complicates inference.* The Spanish item is plausibly *easier* to endorse: "pegar" is a common verb for disciplinary hitting, and disciplinary practices other than "nalgadas" (for example, with an object) may be endorsed. Combined with more normative corporal punishment, this could produce *positive* DIF (higher Spanish endorsement at equal trait), consistent with Alhalel's 28.8% vs 15.9%. This interpretation is my inference from wording and norms, not a tested finding.
- *Response:* Pre-register this item-specific prediction. If confirmed, it shows the DIF method can detect a theoretically predicted, direction-specific effect, which strengthens credibility. Recommend cognitive interviewing or split-ballot follow-up.

**Critique 5C — "Verbal abuse 'le humilló' is stronger than 'put you down'."**
- *Damage: LOW to MEDIUM.* This is a stronger threshold, so it predicts *negative* DIF (lower Spanish endorsement).
- *Response:* Pre-register the direction. This item and physical abuse give opposite-direction predictions, a strong internal falsification test.

**Critique 5D — "For respondents raised abroad, 'served time in prison/jail' and 'illegal street drugs' index different social realities."**
- *Damage: MEDIUM.* Llabre 2017 explicitly raises political imprisonment.
- *Response:* Acknowledge this. Treat incarceration as structurally variable. Without nativity data, language serves as the proxy (see Q6).

**Critique 5E — "The construct is under-represented for immigrants (separation due to migration, deportation, neglect), so equivalence on 11 items doesn't establish construct equivalence."**
- *Damage: MEDIUM. It limits scope rather than invalidating the study.*
- *Response:* Scope the claim to the *measured* items. Note that the 2023 module adds safety and basic-needs items (ACEADSAF and ACEADNED), which could be examined for language DIF where available.

### Gaps
- I found no cognitive-interview or translation-validation study of the BRFSS Spanish ACE module.
- CDC's scoring convention for "Parents not married" was not verified (whether it counts as exposure, non-exposure or missing in official estimates). Published BRFSS ACE studies do not consistently state it; Ford 2014 text was searched and does not mention it.
- I could not verify whether states may modify the Spanish translation (a search-result summary of archived CDC pages suggested so, but the archived page did not load). If true, translation varies by state and is confounded with language effects.
- The Spanish module PDF reviewed is the 2009–2020 version. The 2021+ Spanish wording, including any equivalent of "Not including spanking, (before age 18)," was not retrieved; the 2021 English PDF link returned 404.

## Q6. Confounding and selection: who gets the Spanish interview, is nativity unavailable, and what about differential item nonresponse and break-off?

### Takeaway
Interview language in BRFSS is a bundle of acculturation, nativity, country of childhood, education, age and income. The public BRFSS has no nativity or country-of-birth variable (Singh et al. 2013; no such variable found in the 2023 codebook). So "language DIF" cannot be separated from "raised-abroad DIF." The strongest feasible responses are to restrict the comparison to Hispanic respondents, adjust for other DIF sources simultaneously (MNLFA), use the term "interview-language group," and analyze DK/refusal and break-off by language.

### Cited Findings
- "Large national surveillance systems, such as the Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System and the Youth Risk Behavior Survey, do not include nativity or place-of-birth information for respondents." — [Singh et al. 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3826317/)
- A 2018 review assessed collection of race, ethnicity, language and nativity data across CDC surveillance systems. Its BRFSS-specific coding could not be extracted from the text layer. — [Rodríguez-Lainz et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5805104/)
- Searches of the **2023 BRFSS public codebook** for country of birth, nativity, citizenship and immigration returned no such variable. A search for "Puerto Rican" also returned no Hispanic-origin subgroup variable. QSTLANG is listed as "Language identifier." This was my own text search of the codebook. — [BRFSS 2023 codebook](https://www.cdc.gov/brfss/annual_data/2023/zip/codebook23_llcp-v2-508.zip)
- Spanish-language Hispanics in BRFSS differ sharply in SES and health from English-language Hispanics, and adjustment did not remove language associations. — [DuBard & Gizlice 2008](https://doi.org/10.2105/AJPH.2007.119008)
  - In the BRFSS ACE states (2011–12), Spanish-preference respondents were younger, more male, less educated, lower income and more often uninsured. — [Alhalel 2018](https://prism.northwestern.edu/records/7mt6a-brh38)
- Interview language works better than nativity as an acculturation proxy among the foreign-born (Asian Americans, CHIS). — [Lee, Nguyen & Tsui 2011](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10903-009-9278-z)
- Younger BRFSS respondents report more ACEs. Nonresponders to the ACE module skew young, low-education and low-income. Weighting "does not necessarily adjust for item nonresponse and break offs for optional modules." — [Besera et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11752077/)
- MNLFA lets item parameters depend on several covariates simultaneously. — [Bauer 2017](https://doi.org/10.1037/met0000077)
- ACE prevalence varies strongly within Latinos by heritage: Cuban 20.2% with 4+ ACEs vs higher among Mexican and Puerto Rican heritage. — [Llabre et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5285322/)

### Inferences
**Critique 6A — "Language is confounded with education, age, nativity and acculturation; 'language DIF' could be education DIF or age-cohort DIF."**
- *Strongest sources:* DuBard & Gizlice 2008; Alhalel 2018; Marín 1992 (education moderates response style); Lee et al. 2014 (education moderates context effects).
- *Damage: MEDIUM-HIGH.*
- *Response:*
  - (i) Primary analysis restricted to **Hispanic respondents**, Spanish vs English. This removes race/ethnicity confounding.
  - (ii) **MNLFA/MIMIC with language plus age, sex, education, income, state and survey-year** as simultaneous DIF and impact covariates (Bauer 2017). Report language DIF *net of* DIF attributable to education and age.
  - (iii) Propensity-weighted or matched comparisons as sensitivity analysis.
  - (iv) Label the exposure "interview-language group" and discuss it as a marker of acculturation and country of childhood, not language per se.

**Critique 6B — "Without nativity, you can't tell whether DIF reflects language or having been raised in another country."**
- *Damage: MEDIUM-HIGH and irreducible in public BRFSS.*
- *Response:*
  - (i) Concede it.
  - (ii) Point out that for the *surveillance* question (are Spanish-interview ACE estimates comparable?), the language-group contrast is the operationally relevant one.
  - (iii) Look for state-added nativity questions where available. This is an unverified possibility; see Gaps.
  - (iv) Note that Puerto Rican respondents are US citizens by birth, so language is an even noisier nativity proxy in states with large Puerto Rican populations. This is my inference.

**Critique 6C — "Selection into the Spanish interview is endogenous (respondent choice or interviewer offer), and phone-frame coverage of Spanish-dominant adults differs."**
- *Damage: MEDIUM.* It affects generalizability more than internal DIF validity.
- *Response:* Describe the selection mechanism. Weights do not rake on language. Interpret results as conditional on who completes the interview in each language.

**Critique 6D — "Differential DK/refusal and break-off by language in a late-placed module will bias DIF."**
- *Damage: MEDIUM.* Besera 2024 shows nonresponse rises through the module and peaks on the sexual-abuse items (about 2% refusals in 2023), but did not test language.
- *Response:*
  - (i) Model DK/refused and break-off as outcomes by language, with covariates.
  - (ii) Use FIML/MI with language included (MAR), plus sensitivity analyses that treat refusals as "yes" vs "no" (a pattern-mixture bound).
  - (iii) Report the item-position gradient by language.

**Critique 6E — "Pooling states and years mixes module versions, translations and state compositions with language."**
- *Damage: MEDIUM.* Wording changed in 2021+ (the "Not including spanking" lead-in and two added items). Spanish speakers cluster in particular ACE-module states.
- *Response:* Include state and year (or module-version) fixed effects as DIF/impact covariates. Test invariance across module versions first. Restrict to identical-wording years for the main analysis.

**Critique 6F — "Interviewer effects: Spanish interviews come from a smaller bilingual interviewer pool."**
- *Damage: LOW to MEDIUM, but untestable.* My understanding is that interviewer IDs are not in the public file (see Gaps).
- *Response:* Acknowledge it as a limitation and a component of the "language" contrast.

### Gaps
- Whether any states add nativity or country-of-birth questions to BRFSS in ACE-module years was not verified.
- The availability of interviewer identifiers in BRFSS public files was not verified.
- Whether BRFSS restricted files contain Hispanic origin subgroup (Mexican, Puerto Rican, Cuban, other) was not verified. No such variable was found in the 2023 public codebook text.
- How BRFSS offers or assigns the Spanish interview (respondent request vs interviewer judgment, by state or vendor) was not documented in sources retrieved here.

## Q7. The "so what" objection: how much would plausible DIF change ACE means or 4+ ACE prevalence?

### Takeaway
Precedents show language DIF can be consequential: two-thirds of the MMSE language gap; misleading structural inferences in Latino political surveys. It can also be trivial, as with PROMIS. For ACEs, the practical consequences depend on (a) DIF magnitude on a few items and (b) how close population density sits to the 4+ cutoff. Borsboom et al. 2008 shows that even *invariant* measures produce group-differential classification accuracy at a cutoff when means differ. The study should pre-specify DTF on expected ACE count and on P(4+), and report the DIF-adjusted vs unadjusted language gap, following Jones 2006.

### Cited Findings
- DIF "accounted for two-thirds of the observed difference" between language groups on the MMSE. "Failing to account for measurement differences may lead to spurious inferences." — [Jones 2006](https://doi.org/10.1097/01.mlr.0000245250.50114.0f)
- "Ignoring language DIF can yield misleading inferences about hypothesized relationships between variables." — [Pérez 2011](https://doi.org/10.1093/pan/mpr029)
- PROMIS pain interference: only 1 of 41 items showed language DIF, and English parameters were deemed usable. — [Paz et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11136-017-1499-3)
- DIF consequences "depend on whether or not the patient's performance level approaches the scale decision cutpoint." "Changes in items can affect the percent admitting to a symptom." — [Teresi et al. 2012](https://doi.org/10.1177/0898264312436877)
- DTF "can be more important than DIF when the overall effects of DIF at the test level can be quantified." — [Chalmers, Counsell & Flora 2016](https://doi.org/10.1177/0013164415584576)
- dMACS was linked to "group-level mean differences and adverse impact." — [Nye & Drasgow 2011](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0022955)
- Under measurement invariance with different latent means, sensitivity and positive predictive value are higher in the higher-mean group, and specificity and negative predictive value are higher in the lower-mean group, due to dichotomization. — [Borsboom et al. 2008](https://doi.org/10.1037/1082-989x.13.2.75)
- "Partial invariance may rather effectively recover both path coefficients and latent means even when the majority of items are noninvariant," whereas "recovering latent means and their group rankings might be difficult." — [Pokropek et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1080/10705511.2018.1561293)
- Reference magnitudes: BRFSS 2011–2014 Hispanic mean ACE score 1.80 (95% CI 1.70–1.91); 24.64% overall had 3+ ACEs. — [Merrick et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapediatrics.2018.2537)
  - Physical abuse among Hispanic BRFSS respondents: 28.8% (Spanish) vs 15.9% (English). — [Alhalel 2018](https://prism.northwestern.edu/records/7mt6a-brh38)

### Inferences
**Critique 7A — "Even if DIF exists, it's too small to matter for surveillance."**
- *Damage: MEDIUM.*
- *Worked arithmetic (my inference, using cited numbers):* An ACE count is a sum of binary items, so a DIF-induced shift of d percentage points in one item shifts the mean count by d/100.
  - If *all* of the 12.9-point physical-abuse gap were DIF, the Spanish-group mean would shift by about 0.13 ACEs, roughly 7% of the 1.80 Hispanic mean.
  - Two or three items with 3–5 point DIF in the same direction would shift the mean by about 0.1–0.15.
  - Effects on 4+ prevalence can be proportionally larger or smaller depending on how much mass sits at counts of 3 vs 4. This is the Teresi 2012 "near the cutpoint" point.
- *Response:* Pre-specify the practical-significance criterion. For example: a DIF-adjusted change in the Spanish–English gap in P(4+) of ≥ 2 percentage points, or ≥ 10% of the unadjusted gap, would be policy-relevant. This is my suggested threshold and not a literature standard. Report DTF with uncertainty (Chalmers 2016) and DIF-adjusted vs unadjusted latent and expected-count gaps (Jones 2006 approach).

**Critique 7B — "Null DIF would be uninformative (because of Q2), and positive DIF would be uninterpretable (because of Q5/Q6). Either way the study can't move policy."**
- *Damage: MEDIUM.*
- *Response:* Pre-register directional, item-specific predictions in *opposite directions* (physical abuse positive, verbal abuse and sexual abuse negative, divorce structural). Confirmation of a pattern is informative regardless of the uniform-shift ambiguity. Also report impact on the *other* main uses of BRFSS ACEs: dose-response associations by language (path coefficients are better recovered under partial invariance; Pokropek 2019).

### Gaps
- I found no published study quantifying how ACE item DIF changes ACE count means or 4+ prevalence for any group.
- The distribution of ACE counts specifically among Spanish-interview BRFSS respondents (mass at 3 vs 4) was not available. It is needed to calculate cutoff sensitivity.

## Q8 (overall). Which critiques are most damaging, what other critiques were not in the original list, and which analytic plan best survives them?

### Takeaway
The ranking from most to least damaging: (1) pervasive/uniform DIF is unidentifiable from internal data; (2) DIF may reflect real conditional differences (divorce, incarceration, corporal punishment) rather than reporting bias, and without nativity language cannot be separated from childhood abroad; (3) the reflective-model premise, if the research question keeps "same underlying adversity"; (4) reliance on ΔCFI under severely unequal group sizes; (5) small and sparse focal-group cells for sexual-abuse and incarceration items. All are mitigable to a defensible level with a reframed estimand, MNLFA/IRT item-level DIF with effect sizes and DTF, anchor-sensitivity analyses, pre-registered direction-specific item hypotheses, and external triangulation. Critique (1) can be bounded but not eliminated.

### Cited Findings
- **Precedent BRFSS ACE invariance work exists but ignores language:** Ford 2014 (sex, age; configural and metric); Mei 2022 (six groupings; claims scalar invariance). Jensen 2024 (NSCH) found threshold noninvariance across race/ethnicity. — [Ford et al. 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4587306/); [Mei et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9706892/); [Jensen et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11023799/)
- **Other recent ACE invariance studies:**
  - A Justice Quarterly 2025 study (n=993 young women) reported ACE invariance across White, Black and Hispanic groups. — [Justice Quarterly 2025](https://doi.org/10.1080/07418825.2025.2451311)
  - Fragile Families/FFCWS factor analysis tested invariance by birth poverty. — [Moore et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1177/08862605251355626)
  - Prakash et al. (2026) call for invariance testing in minoritized groups. — [Prakash et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1002/jts.70097)
- **Retrospective-report validity is weak overall:** κ=0.19 (Baldwin 2019). False negatives dominate and false positives are rare (Hardt & Rutter 2004). — [Baldwin et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6551848/); [Hardt & Rutter 2004](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1469-7610.2004.00218.x)
- **Methodological reviews and state of the art:** measurement invariance in the social sciences (historical development, challenges, state of the art); cross-national equivalence; reporting conventions. — [Leitgöb et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ssresearch.2022.102805); [Davidov et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-soc-071913-043137); [Putnick & Bornstein 2016](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.dr.2016.06.004)
- **Partial invariance:** the classic partial invariance paper; the 80% sufficient-partial-invariance rule attributed to Dimitrov 2010 (as used by Jensen 2024). — [Byrne, Shavelson & Muthén 1989](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.105.3.456); [Dimitrov 2010](https://doi.org/10.1177/0748175610373459)
  - Forward specification search vs approximate invariance for adjusting latent parameters. — [Lai, Liu & Tse 2022](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13428-021-01560-2)
  - Many-group comparisons of five MI approaches. — [Kim et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1080/10705511.2017.1304822)

### Inferences
**Additional critiques not in the original list (summary with damage ratings):**
1. *Retrospective recall error dominates.* Agreement with prospective data is poor (κ=0.19), and the error is mainly false negatives. Differential false negatives by language is precisely the uniform-shift scenario. **MEDIUM.** Response: scope the study to "reported" ACEs and cite Hardt & Rutter on error direction.
2. *Multidimensionality and local dependence* (three sexual-abuse items; household cluster) produce spurious DIF in unidimensional models. **MEDIUM.** Response: three-factor, bifactor or testlet IRT; collapse sexual abuse.
3. *Module version and translation heterogeneity across years and states* (2021+ wording; 13 items in 2023; possible state translation changes, unverified). **MEDIUM.** Response: version-homogeneous pooling; state and year covariates.
4. *Within-Hispanic heterogeneity* (Cuban vs Mexican vs Puerto Rican; Puerto Ricans are US citizens) with no origin variable in the public file. **MEDIUM.** Response: state fixed effects as a crude proxy; acknowledge.
5. *Category-use and response-style effects* on frequency items (Marín 1992). **LOW-MEDIUM.** Response: graded models.
6. *Interviewer effects confounded with language.* **LOW-MEDIUM, untestable publicly.**
7. *Differential prediction/outcome DIF trap* if outcomes like self-rated health are used for validation (Viruell-Fuentes 2011). **MEDIUM if used.** Response: language-robust outcomes only.
8. *Novelty overclaim.* The Alhalel 2018 thesis already compared BRFSS ACE item prevalence by questionnaire language (marginal, not DIF). **LOW**, but cite it to pre-empt reviewers.

**Recommended analytic plan that best survives the critiques (synthesis):**
- **Estimand.** Item-level reporting equivalence across interview-language groups, conditional on standing on the remaining items. State explicitly that uniform shifts are not identified.
- **Sample.** Pooled BRFSS years with identical ACE wording. Primary: Hispanic respondents, Spanish vs English (QSTLANG 2 vs 1; exclude "Other"). Secondary: all respondents.
- **Pre-registration.** Item-specific directional hypotheses:
  - physical abuse: positive DIF in Spanish (wording plus norms);
  - verbal abuse: negative ("humilló");
  - sexual abuse and household mental illness: negative (disclosure/stigma);
  - divorce: structural, with "not married" handling;
  - incarceration: structural.
  Also pre-register a sensitivity-gradient test.
- **Primary model.** MNLFA or multi-group 2PL/graded IRT within a three-factor or bifactor structure, with language plus age, sex, education, income, state and year as DIF and impact covariates (Bauer 2017).
- **DIF identification and sensitivity.** Use theory-based anchors, lasso-regularized DIF (Belzak & Bauer 2020), robust scaling (Halpin 2024), and alignment or Bayesian approximate invariance. Report the range of language-gap estimates across identification schemes (Robitzsch & Lüdtke 2023).
- **Converging item-level checks.**
  - lordif ordinal LR DIF: Nagelkerke ΔR² ≥ .02 or β1 change ≥ 5–10%.
  - Mantel–Haenszel with ETS A/B/C, using refined rest-score matching.
  - If MGCFA is reported: WLSMV with Wu–Estabrook identification, ΔCFI ≥ −.004 plus ΔRMSEA ≤ .01 (Rutkowski & Svetina 2017), and equivalence tests (Yuan & Chan 2016).
- **Effect sizes and impact.** dMACS (≥.20 small, ≥.40 medium, ≥.70 large; Nye et al. 2019), IRT expected-score differences (Meade 2010; Steinberg & Thissen 2006), and DTF/DRF on expected ACE count and on P(4+) (Chalmers 2016, 2018). Report DIF-adjusted vs unadjusted language gaps (Jones 2006).
- **Inference.** BH-FDR across items and DIF types (Thissen et al. 2002). Use design-based estimation with weights, strata and PSU (Asparouhov 2005; Ford 2014), plus an unweighted sensitivity analysis.
- **Sparse data.** Collapse or testlet the sexual-abuse items. Use Firth-penalized or Bayesian logistic models (Firth 1993; Heinze & Schemper 2002). Make nonuniform DIF exploratory.
- **Missingness.** Model DK/refusal and break-off by language, and use bounding sensitivity analyses (extending Besera 2024).
- **Formative and descriptive sensitivity.** A causal-indicator MIMIC model with slope-invariance tests (Diamantopoulos & Papadopoulos 2010; Moriarity & Slavich 2025), composite vs reflective comparisons (Loncar 2026), and covariate-adjusted item prevalence differences (Rohrer & Paulewicz 2025).
- **External triangulation.** Compare the direction and size of the language/nativity gap with NSCH parent report (Caballero 2017; Loria 2018), HCHS/SOL (Llabre 2017) and NESARC (Vaughn 2017). Propose follow-up experiments: randomized language among bilinguals, split-ballot wording of the Spanish physical- and verbal-abuse items, and anchoring vignettes for threshold-type items.

### Gaps
- No published BRFSS ACE language DIF/invariance study was found (PubMed, Europe PMC, OpenAlex searches as of 2026-11-01). The only direct comparison is the unpublished Alhalel 2018 thesis, which compares marginal prevalences.
- No ACE-specific simulation evidence exists on the performance of anchor-free DIF methods under the mixed-direction, few-item, low-prevalence conditions expected here. Analysts should run their own simulations calibrated to BRFSS prevalences.
