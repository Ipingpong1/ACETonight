## Q1. Are there NEW (2025–2026) papers, preprints, dissertations or conference abstracts on ACE measurement invariance/DIF by language, nativity, acculturation or Hispanic ethnicity, or on under-reporting of the household mental illness, drug use or incarceration items?

### Takeaway
No. Nothing published, preprinted, deposited or presented through 2 November 2026 tests ACE item functioning by interview or survey language, nativity or acculturation [NF].

The 2025–2026 ACE-measurement literature groups respondents by other variables:
- race/ethnicity (Krushas et al. 2025; Umoga 2026);
- sexual orientation and gender identity (Dorri et al. 2026);
- poverty (Moore et al. 2026);
- or no groups at all, scoring items with IRT or reliability statistics only (Zhang & Lippert 2026; the All of Us validation preprint).

One Spanish-vs-English ACE item comparison is newly found: a withdrawn 2019 SSWR abstract (LaBrenz et al.). It used unconditional chi-square tests and found no language difference on the household items.

### Inferences
- The field moved toward invariance testing in 2025–2026, but along race/ethnicity, sexual/gender identity and poverty, not language. The language gap identified on 2026-11-01 is still open on 2026-11-02.
- LaBrenz et al. 2019 is the only other US Spanish-vs-English ACE item comparison found, besides Alhalel 2018. Its null result on the household items deserves one sentence in the Discussion, but carries little weight:
  - the sample was small, help-seeking, mostly female and very low-income;
  - the tests were unconditional;
  - the abstract was withdrawn.
- All of Us now holds a BRFSS-derived 11-item ACE module. If it records the language in which surveys were completed (not verified), it could serve as a third replication dataset.

## Q2. What exactly do Zhang & Lippert 2026, Krushas et al. 2025, Dorri et al. 2026 and Umoga 2026 test, and does any of them test language or nativity?

### Takeaway
On the most detailed material obtainable, none of the four tests language or nativity.

The closest are Zhang & Lippert (2PL IRT scoring of BRFSS ACE items) and Umoga (multigroup LCA of BRFSS ACE items by race/ethnicity). Both use BRFSS ACE items and item-level parameters, but neither abstract mentions DIF by language.

### Inferences
- **Zhang & Lippert** are the only other group fitting an IRT model to BRFSS ACE items in 2026. Their abstract describes calibration and scoring ("item-level differences in adversity severity"), not DIF.
  - Because the full text is unread, the manuscript should not assert that they did not examine DIF. It can say that "their published abstract reports IRT scoring without group-specific item parameters".
  - **Action before submission:** open the full text through NYU Libraries (ScienceDirect) and search it for "DIF", "invariance", "Spanish", "language" and "QSTLANG".
- **Umoga's Study 1** is the closest *pattern* precedent. In BRFSS 2021–2023, cross-group heterogeneity is concentrated in exactly the household mental illness and household incarceration indicators, plus alcohol misuse.
  - Hispanic adults are one of the six groups. In the researcher's BRFSS 2019–2025 sample, about 31% of Hispanic ACE respondents (10,945 of 35,418) were interviewed in Spanish. Part of Umoga's Hispanic heterogeneity could therefore be the language effect that H1 isolates.
  - The two studies are complementary: race/ethnicity with a person-centred LCA, versus language with item-level DIF conditional on the other items.
  - Because the full text is embargoed, a language sensitivity analysis inside it cannot be excluded. Contacting the author (or her advisor, Shoba Ramanadhan; prior session) is the only way to close this.
- **Krushas et al. and Dorri et al.** are not threats to novelty. Their populations, instruments or groupings differ, and neither compares languages.

## Q3. Is there any CHIS ACE psychometric or language-related work, and any NSCH work testing ACE item functioning by household language or nativity?

### Takeaway
No psychometric study of the CHIS ACE items exists, and no NSCH study tests ACE item functioning by household language or nativity [NF].

The only invariance precedents in these surveys are:
- **Kim et al. 2016:** CHIS, IRT-DIF of the K6 distress scale by interview language; not ACEs.
- **Jensen et al. 2024:** NSCH, ACE thresholds by race/ethnicity.

New detail from Jensen's final model shows that NSCH caregiver reports do **not** show the H1 pattern at the ethnicity level.

### Cited Findings
**CHIS**
- **All CHIS-ACE papers retrieved are association studies.** PubMed returned 9; Europe PMC 48 (3 of them with "Spanish" or "language of interview" in the full text); OpenAlex 11. [ABS/titles]
  - Kotz et al. 2026 (2021 CHIS, n = 24,453)
  - Chwa et al. 2026 (Asian Americans; English proficiency as a covariate)
  - Haro-Ramos et al. 2026 (Latinos aged 50+; citizenship)
  - Jang & Gonzales 2025 (Asian American generations)
  - Pereira & Zhu 2025

  - a 2025 CSUSB thesis on ACEs and substance use (titles only)

  - None examines measurement equivalence. — [Chwa 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41709070/); [Kotz 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42628986/); [Haro-Ramos 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41631104/); [Le 2026](https://doi.org/10.1002/wmh3.70062)
- **No CHIS cognitive-testing or translation report for the ACE section was located** [NF]. - **CHIS method precedent (not ACE): Kim, DeCoster, Bryant & Ford 2016.** IRT-DIF of the K6 among 44,846 CHIS adults. [ABS] — [PubMed 26282779](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26282779/)
  - Four items varied across races/ethnicities, and four ("nervous," "hopeless," "restless," "depressed") varied across languages.
  - In effect-size analyses, "the Hispanic/Latino Spanish group was not different from the Hispanic/Latino English group." The White-English group differed from both Hispanic groups.
- **Also: Shon 2020.** CHIS 2012, K6 equivalence between Chinese and Korean immigrants. [ABS] — [PubMed 32172536](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32172536/)

**NSCH**
- **Jensen, Bernard & Lanier 2024 final partial-scalar model** (NSCH 2018–2020; caregiver report; single factor; WLSMV) [FT: tables read]. — [PMC11023799](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11023799/)
  - Household mental illness (ace8): threshold constrained equal across all five groups (τ = 1.33 for NH White, NH Black, NH AI/AN, NH Asian and Hispanic).
  - Parent/guardian served time in jail (ace5): τ = 1.42 for Hispanic vs 1.60 for NH White. A lower threshold means the item is endorsed at lower latent adversity.
  - Alcohol/drug problem (ace9): τ = 1.30 for Hispanic vs 1.28 for NH White.
  - Unweighted endorsement, Hispanic vs NH White: mental illness 9% vs 10%; jail 8% vs 5%; alcohol/drugs 10% vs 10%.
  - The word "language" never occurs in the article.
- **NSCH household-language and nativity papers remain prevalence or association studies:**
  - Campbell & Jacobs 2026 [FT, prior session]
  - Chipalo et al. 2026 [ABS, prior session]
  - Uddin et al. 2026: parental-nativity gaps in parental imprisonment narrowed over 2016–2023 [ABS]
  - Loria & Caughy 2018: low-income Latino children, NSCH 2011–2012; ≥2 ACEs in 13% of immigrant vs 40% of non-immigrant children [ABS]
  - Links: [Campbell & Jacobs 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13083318/); [Uddin 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42949415/); [Loria & Caughy 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29246345/)
- **Europe PMC full-text query** (NSCH + ACE + invariance/DIF): 1 record, Zhang & Monnat 2022 (racial/ethnic ACE clusters, LCA). No language analysis [NF for language DIF]. — [Zhang & Monnat 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34984220/)

### Inferences
- **Components (b) and (e) are uncontested.** The CHIS ACE items have never been examined psychometrically, and the only CHIS DIF-by-language precedent concerns a distress scale (Kim 2016). Kim 2016 is still a useful methodological citation: it separated race/ethnicity from language by using English-interview Hispanic respondents as a bridge group, the same design logic as H1.
- **Jensen's thresholds matter for framing (c).** At the ethnicity level, in parent-proxy, self-administered NSCH data, Hispanic caregivers show no raised threshold for household mental illness and a *lower* threshold for jail.
  - If H1 holds in BRFSS and CHIS, the under-endorsement is specific to Spanish-language administration (and/or adult retrospective self-report), not a general Hispanic reporting style. This supports language, rather than ethnicity, as the operative grouping.
  - Caveats: NSCH Hispanic respondents mix English and Spanish completions, and NSCH asks about the child's current household rather than the respondent's own childhood.

### Gaps
- The share of NSCH Hispanic respondents who complete the survey in Spanish is not public (prior session), so Jensen's Hispanic thresholds cannot be split by language.
- No CHIS methodology report on Spanish pretesting of the ACE items was found. Whether CHIS cognitively tested "¿Vivió con alguien que estaba deprimido/a, era enfermo/a mental o suicida?" is unknown.

## Q4. Spanish- and Portuguese-language literature (SciELO, Redalyc, Dialnet, LILACS/BVS): is there language or translation-equivalence work on ACE or ACE-IQ items, especially the household mental illness or incarceration items?

### Takeaway
Only single-language adaptation and validation studies exist: Brazil, Chile, Colombia, Mexico, Ecuador and Spain, plus a Spanish ACE-IQ tested with Latine immigrants in Virginia. Several document semantic equivalence through translation, back-translation and pretesting.

None of them [NF]:
- compares a Spanish or Portuguese version with an English version empirically;
- reports a problem specific to the household mental illness items ("enfermo mental", "deprimido", "suicida") or to the incarceration item.

Programmatic searches of SciELO, LILACS/BVS and Redalyc were blocked; Dialnet partly worked.

### Cited Findings
- **Pereira et al. 2021** (*Rev Saúde Pública* 55:79): ACE-IQ cross-cultural adaptation for Brazil. [FT: keyword-searched] — [PMC9586440](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9586440/)
  - Method: semantic equivalence through two translations, two back-translations, content validation by eight health workers, and pretests "to assess acceptability, understanding and emotional impact".
  - Result: "the adapted version proved to be easy to apply and to understand and achieved good semantic equivalence."
  - Household items as rendered: "Você morou com alguém que estava deprimido, ou tinha alguma doença mental ou intenção suicida?" and "Você morou com alguém que alguma vez tenha sido levado pra cadeia ou mandado pra prisão?"
  - No item-specific difficulty with these items appeared in the passages matched by keyword search.
- **Kuperman et al. 2024** (*Cad Saúde Colet*): a second Brazilian ACE-IQ adaptation, with pregnant women in Rio de Janeiro. Conceptual, semantic and operational equivalence were assessed. After the first pretest, seven items were adapted, including three "ambiente familiar" (household environment) items. The content validity index reached 0.89–1 by the third pretest. The abstract does not say which household items changed. [ABS] — [doi:10.1590/1414-462x202432010257](https://doi.org/10.1590/1414-462x202432010257)
- **Swingen 2020** (VCU PhD dissertation): the ACE-IQ was translated into Spanish and given to 184 adult English-language learners (Latine immigrants) in Chesterfield County, Virginia. [ABS via fetch summary] — [VCU Scholars Compass etd/6187](https://scholarscompass.vcu.edu/etd/6187)
  - Results: α = .908; a three-factor CFA; concurrent validity with the BRFSS ACE items (r² = .862).
  - There was no Spanish–English comparison, invariance test or DIF test, and no item-level household results are reported in the abstract.
- **Other single-language work:**
  - Reyes-Gaspar et al. 2023: Colombian ACE version; 16 items in four factors, including sociopolitical violence; 100 students [ABS]
  - Peñaloza-Díaz et al. 2026: Escala Integral de Experiencias Adversas (Chile; 115 students) [ABS]
  - Villatoro-Rodríguez et al. 2025: Spanish PEARLS validation (*Rev Colomb Psiquiatr*) [ABS: title only]
  - From the prior session: Villanueva 2026 (Spain), Casas-Muñoz 2024 (Mexico), Santelices 2025 (Chile), Ramírez 2025/2026 (Ecuador) and the 2026 Ecuador pilot.
  - Links: [Reyes-Gaspar 2023](https://doi.org/10.22463/17949831.3882); [Peñaloza-Díaz 2026](https://doi.org/10.21865/ridep79.1.03); [Villatoro-Rodríguez 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.rcp.2025.01.006)
- **Database access:**
  - SciELO search (search.scielo.org) and BVS/LILACS (pesquisa.bvsalud.org) returned HTTP 403 to programmatic requests.
  - Redalyc's search returned a 5.6 KB JavaScript shell with no parseable results.
  - Dialnet reset four of six connections. Of the two queries that returned, "experiencias adversas infancia funcionamiento diferencial" gave 4 titles, none relevant, and "experiencias adversas infancia hispanos idioma" gave 0.
  - SciELO, Redalyc and LILACS content was therefore reached indirectly: through OpenAlex (Spanish terms, 23 hits; Portuguese terms, 36 hits) and a `site:scielo.org` web search. None of these returned cross-language equivalence or DIF work [NF].

### Inferences
- **The Latin American literature treats translation equivalence as a qualitative, pre-psychometric step** (translation and back-translation, expert panels, pretests). No study tests whether the translated household items *function* the same as the English ones.
  - Given the database caveats below, this statement is defensible: "no study has empirically tested the cross-language equivalence of the household mental illness or incarceration ACE items in any Spanish or Portuguese translation."
- **Wording:** in all the translations seen (Brazilian ACE-IQ, CHIS and BRFSS) the mental illness item compounds depression, mental illness and suicidality (e.g., "doença mental ou intenção suicida"). Lexical stigma attached to "enfermo/a mental" or "doença mental" is a plausible contributor to DIF, but it is untested.

- SciELO, LILACS/BVS and Redalyc could not be searched directly. A manual browser search is advisable before submission:

## Q5. Adjacent evidence: are specific ACE household items under-reported because of stigma or disclosure barriers, and has anyone framed "stigmatized household conditions" as a DIF or reporting mechanism?

### Takeaway
Three strands of evidence bear on the mechanism, all indirect:
- **Item-specific informant evidence:** household mental illness is the household item most prone to proxy under-reporting.
- **General survey-methods evidence:** questions on incarceration and drug use attract under-reporting driven by social desirability.
- **Prevalence evidence:** immigrant and earlier-generation Latinos report fewer household-dysfunction ACEs.

**No study frames "stigmatized household conditions" as a DIF or reporting mechanism for ACE items.** The phrase does not occur in PubMed or Europe PMC, and a web search found no ACE usage [NF].

There is also counter-evidence, most notably very high endorsement of these items in the mostly Spanish-preferring HCHS/SOL cohort.

### Inferences
- **The framing is original.** No source found labels the three items "stigmatized household conditions" or predicts a coherent negative-DIF cluster for them. The supporting evidence is indirect, so the manuscript should present it as a hypothesis-generating rationale, not an established mechanism:
  - household mental illness shows the largest proxy-informant gap among household items (Ng 2025);
  - living with a mentally ill relative carries affiliate stigma (Goldberg 2023);
  - incarceration and drug use are classic sensitive questions (Yan & Cantor 2019; Fendrich & Johnson 2005);
  - ACEs themselves attract public stigma (Purtle 2022);
  - earlier immigrant generations report fewer household-dysfunction ACEs, especially substance use (Grest 2021).
- **Two findings complicate a pure stigma story, and reviewers may raise both.**
  1. **HCHS/SOL.** In a cohort that was 75% Spanish-preferring, mostly foreign-born and interviewed face to face, endorsement was about 20% for household mental illness, about 30% for substance use and about 25% for prison. These rates are at or above the BRFSS English-interview Hispanic rates, so Spanish-speaking Hispanic adults *do* endorse these items in some designs. Other factors may matter more than language per se:
     - context and mode: in-person bilingual interviewers in a research cohort, using the CDC–Kaiser wording;
     - sample composition, e.g., political imprisonment among Cuban and Central American immigrants;
     - translation.

     HCHS/SOL prevalence is not broken down by language, so this is not a direct contradiction.
  2. **Incarceration is stable.** Its test–retest agreement is very high (κ = 0.93 in Page 2026). That does not rule out stable concealment, but it shows the item is not inherently ambiguous.
- **Incarceration is a less clean stigma case than mental illness.** For people raised abroad, the incarceration item may index different realities, such as political imprisonment (Llabre 2017). The nativity-adjusted CHIS result for household mental illness is therefore the strongest evidence for a language-specific reporting mechanism.

### Gaps
- No study was found that measures disclosure barriers specifically for the household mental illness or incarceration items among Spanish-speaking adults, for example:
  - randomized self- vs interviewer-administration;
  - list experiments;
  - cognitive interviews in Spanish on these items.
- The Fendrich & Johnson 2005 full text (PMC3455900) was not read for Hispanic-specific results.
- Item-level kappas for the household items in Dube et al. 2004 (the classic ACE test–retest study) were not retrieved this session.
- Goldberg 2023 and Dobener 2022 were identified by title only.

### Inferences
#### Recommended novelty sentence (Introduction or Discussion)
> "To our knowledge, no previous study has tested whether adverse childhood experience (ACE) items function differently by interview language. Prior work has compared ACE item prevalence by questionnaire language among Hispanic BRFSS respondents without conditioning on overall adversity (Alhalel, 2018); tested measurement invariance across racial and ethnic groups but not language (Mei et al., 2022; Jensen et al., 2024; Krushas et al., 2025; Umoga, 2026); or tested Spanish–English invariance only for a broader lifetime-adversity model in a predominantly Spanish-speaking immigrant sample (Cooper & Nickodem, 2021). This study is the first to estimate item-level differential item functioning of ACE items by interview language among Hispanic adults in two population surveys with independently translated Spanish instruments (BRFSS 2019–2025 and CHIS 2021–2025), and to test whether language-related differences persist after adjustment for nativity."

Shorter variant (Abstract):
> "This is the first study to test differential item functioning of ACE items by interview language, replicated across two U.S. population surveys with independent Spanish translations."

Hedges to keep:
- Use "to our knowledge", and add "literature searched through 2 November 2026" in Methods or a supplement.
- Do not write "first to show that Spanish speakers under-report household ACEs"; Alhalel 2018 and Grest 2021 already show lower *marginal* reporting.
- Write "stigmatized household conditions" as the authors' a priori classification, with stigma as a hypothesized mechanism.
- Avoid "no study has examined ACEs by language"; that is false (Alhalel 2018; LaBrenz et al. 2019; Campbell & Jacobs 2026; Chipalo et al. 2026).
- Cite Zhang & Lippert 2026 only for IRT scoring until their full text has been checked.

#### Adversarial reading: how a reviewer could use each closest work against the novelty claim, and the response
- **Objection:** "Alhalel already showed that Spanish-preference respondents report fewer household ACEs."
  **Response:** Alhalel compared marginal prevalence in 2,983 respondents (2011–12, 9 states) and found no difference in the ACE-count distribution. That is exactly the situation in which DIF, rather than true exposure, has to be tested, and H1 conditions on the other items.
- **Objection:** "Cooper & Nickodem already tested Spanish vs English invariance."
  **Response:** Their test covered a 59-item lifetime-adversity bifactor model in an all-immigrant sample that was 92% Spanish (about 8% English), with no ACE item-level DIF. Their partial-scalar result is consistent with, and motivates, an item-level test.
- **Objection:** "Umoga already found BRFSS heterogeneity in the same items."
  **Response:** Umoga's heterogeneity is across race/ethnicity, from a person-centred LCA, and is unpublished and embargoed. H1 isolates language within Hispanic adults and conditions on latent adversity. Cite Umoga as convergent evidence.
- **Objection:** "Jensen found no Hispanic threshold shift for household mental illness."
  **Response:** NSCH is a caregiver proxy report on current household exposures, self-administered, and does not separate Spanish from English completions. The contrast supports an effect specific to language rather than to ethnicity.
- **Objection:** "HCHS/SOL's Spanish-preferring adults endorse these items at high rates."
  **Response:** HCHS/SOL used different wording, in-person administration and a different sampling frame, and did not tabulate by language. Note it as a limitation and as an avenue for replication in HCHS/SOL.
